Where was this taken?

A photo, video or audio clip usually reveals where it was made through several independent channels. Each one narrows the search a different amount. Investigators combine them: one rules out a continent, another picks a valley, a third picks the street.

Use this on your own media, or for verification and journalism. These are standard open-source investigation (OSINT) techniques. Using them to find a private person's home is stalking. Nothing you load on this page is uploaded; all processing stays in your browser.

2 · Sun and shadows chronolocation, in reverselive demo

At any instant the sun stands straight overhead at one point on Earth, the subsolar point. A shadow's length gives the sun's elevation angle, and every place that sees the sun at that elevation lies on a circle around the subsolar point. If you also know which compass direction the shadow points, only one spot on that circle fits.

(same units)

Sun position from standard astronomical formulas (accurate to about 0.1°, ignoring refraction). In practice the time comes from metadata, a clock in frame, or a known event; an error of 4 minutes moves the answer by 1° of longitude.

3 · Skyline and terrain the shape of the horizonlive demo

Mountains and ridges form a horizon line that is close to unique for each viewpoint. Researchers render the horizon from a digital elevation model at many candidate points and compare it with the ridge line traced from a photo. 4 · Mains hum electrical network frequencylive demo

Recordings made near mains power often pick up a faint hum at the grid frequency and its harmonics: 50 Hz across Europe, Africa, most of Asia and Australia, and 60 Hz across North America, much of South America, Taiwan, South Korea and western Japan. The frequency also wanders by a few hundredths of a hertz, in step across a whole grid. Forensic labs match that wander against logged grid data to recover when a recording was made, and on which grid.

5 · Aircraft Doppler planes you can't hear

Small propeller planes leave faint engine tones that slide down in pitch as they pass. Each slide gives the plane's speed and closest distance, and public flight tracking (ADS-B) says which planes were where. This is the experiment on the main page, rebuilt from scratch.

We also tested the detector on 10 hours of audio from a public webcam with a known location (2026-11-08, 13:00–23:00 UTC). It found 3 of the 5 propeller planes that ADS-B logged within 8 km. The best match, a Pilatus PC-12 turboprop, was heard 1.5 s from the predicted time, at 141.7 m/s against 141.9 m/s from ADS-B and 4.7 km against 5.0 km. 6 · Visual matching what a photo shows

The most common approach for photos: compare what is visible with imagery whose location is already known.

Retrieval against street imagery

Build a database of street-level photos with known positions (Google Street View, open Mapillary or KartaView), turn every image into a compact descriptor, and look up the nearest matches for the query photo. Accurate where coverage is dense. The cost is the reference set: commercial imagery is billed per image, so wide searches get expensive quickly.

Learned geolocation models

Models trained on millions of geotagged photos (PlaNet, GeoCLIP, PIGEON) predict a location straight from pixels, using vegetation, road markings, architecture, light and sky. Usually right about the country, sometimes the city; street-level only with a retrieval step on top.

Signs, text and infrastructure

Language and script, phone number formats, licence plates, bollards, utility poles, road-line colours, which side of the road traffic drives on. Manual, but very effective; it is how competitive GeoGuessr players work.

Sky and weather

Star fields and the moon's phase fix a latitude and a date; cloud and rain can be checked against archived weather radar and satellite images for the claimed time and place.
